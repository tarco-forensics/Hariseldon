THE MEANING OF ECONOMICS
AND ECONOMIC POLITICS
Determining how to intervene in the economy in order to change
"what it is" in favour of "what it should be" requires clarifying its con-
cepts. The different definitions of economic science are linked or condi-
tioned by the historical moment and by different schools and doctrines.
The reality is that there is no widely accepted definition. For many new
generation economists, economics is everything in society; some go so
far as to consider that even breathing implies economic activity, while
others think that a market is necessary for economic activity to exist.
This led Myrdal to say that the only concept an economist does not need
to define is that of economic science.
There are many examples of how fruitful the debate on economics
has been. Thus, for example, we have Smith, who focuses political eco-
nomy on the achievement of income for families and the State or Repu-
blic, and who seeks abundant income for the former and sufficient inco-
me for the latter. For his part, Mill constructs a homo economicus with
an aversion to work and a desire to cover his costly desires for goods
and services, so he sees economics as an abstract science that deduces its
postulates from the intention of obtaining the maximum amount of
goods and services, whether necessary or superfluous, with the least
possible amount of work and physical sacrifice, given the degree of
knowledge attained. Marx focuses his concept from a scientific perspec-
tive and by means of laws emanating from the production and exchange
of the material means of subsistence in which man participates. For Je-
vons, economic science studies the conditions that human behaviour
must satisfy in order to obtain maximum pleasure at minimum cost in
the form of hardship. Veblen, finally, invites us to a walk through a

economics, understood as the science that studies the allocation of scarce
means between alternative uses. However, he studies economic reality
as it is, not as it should be, so that economics arise at the moment when

THE EXERCISE OF INTERVENTION
AND LIMITS TO THE PUBLIC SECTOR
Public sector intervention in the market is usually justified by the
existence of market failures, so that politics is intended to correct dys-
functionalities in the system, such as the lack of genuine competition,
which implies that the market sets up barriers to the proper functioning
of the market economy; the externalities produced by some activities,
which make it advisable for their costs to be shared with society; there
are imperfections in time and space, in the functioning of sectors, in in-
ternational relations, in the allocation of resources; the distribution of
results does not allow for adequate levels of capital accumulation, de-
preciation or purchasing power needed in the economy, so that correcti-
ve or boosting factors have to be introduced, etc.
In the face of these and other possible market failures, economic po-
litics is necessary. Economic politics is subsidiary to the market, since the
market does not function perfectly. The state thus acquires the well-
known four functions in its intervention in the economy: stabilisation,
regulation and control, allocation of resources and redistribution. In
practice, all economic politics measures have stabilising, allocative, dis-
tributive, regulatory and control impacts; but the intentionality that
marks an economic politics strategy is that which prioritises one of the
functions over the others.
In short, all this translates into a series of monetary, fiscal and bud-
getary, exchange rate, direct controls and structural reform policies,
which, depending on the approach marked by the objective pursued,
will be closer to one or another function of the state, although in most
cases, all policies have a multiple effect, allocating resources, distribu-
ting results, stabilising the economic cycle and regulating or controlling
the economic activity of individuals in the market.

Although economic politics is traditionally dealt with from a natio-
nal perspective, the territory of application of an economic politics stra-
tegy or the territorial influence on a specific action is increasingly begin-
ning to take on very different meanings to that of the nation-state,
although not in all countries the circumstances are common, and the
control of most economic politics competencies continues to remain un-
der the same national prism. In the current context, three phenomena are
growing in parallel and all of them enhance the role to be played by
movement, effort and trust in the functioning of the economic system:
the decentralisation of the economy, its globalisation (understood as an
accentuation of the process of internationalisation of the different hu-
man activities) and integration (particularly impacting on economic po-
litics insofar as it advances in terms of supranationalisation). This is to
be specified in terms of the following phenomena:
1. Globalisation and its impact on economic politics. Apart from the per-
sistence of protectionist and liberalising measures that coexist to varying
degrees depending on the territory and the time, the trend that is emer-
ging is globalisation, which is gaining momentum, beyond the control of
economies, thanks to the influence that new technologies exert on the
process, making it impossible to remain isolated from the phenomenon,
except to the serious detriment of citizens.
The power derived from international external sources is growing,
usually in the form of constraints that force the development of certain
economic politics measures on a territory. The strength of this influence
depends on the importance of foreign trade, the level of its economy
and its balance of payments situation. From these three characteristics,

it follows that it is the more advanced economies that are in a better
position to influence economic politics than the less advanced ones. In-
ternational cooperation and European integration policies are good
examples of the high degree of international influence on national poli-
tics-making.
On the other hand, one of the basic characteristics of the globalisa-
tion process being experienced is that it lacks leadership and manage-
ment; there are no adequate leadership and management bodies to try
to exploit the advantages, disseminate them to all participants and co-
rrect the disadvantages. This globalisation requires a strong dose of coo-
peration and coordination, and appropriate mechanisms for its manage-
ment are needed. At the moment, there is only improvisation, under
traditional control assurance prisms, which in this case are not bearing
the desired and necessary fruit.
In the business sphere, many of the large traditional companies are
opting for size and business merger-absorption as a defence mechanism
in the face of globalisation, trying to gain control over the market in or-

establish greater legislative controls on a world of increasingly free mo-
vement: improvisation as opposed to acceptance of the process and wor-
king together to better define the process, always favouring movement,
effort and trust.
2. Supranationalisation, coordination and cooperation in economic poli-
tics. Faced with the situation of globalisation described above, "the states
constituted in the modern era reveal their exhaustion, their growing in-

by the internationalisation of economic, social and cultural forces" (Su-
biros, 1992). Supranational integration processes, such as the European
one, thus appear as the response of national systems to the challenge
posed by these economic agents with a planetary competitive logic. In
this sense, institutions with objectives of international cooperation and
the functioning of the global world system also emerge.
In recent decades, following the rapid spread of the phenomenon of
globalisation, the world economy has become more interrelated and the
different states that make it up have developed different formulas for
organising relations between them. Economic cooperation, coordination
and integration are currently the main formulas for relations between
states.

3. Decentralisation of economic politics decisions. Although the appli-
cation of economic politics was born with a clear vision of the national
territorial scope, the truth is that as the needs of economic and social
agents have demanded it, economic politics actions have been changing
their territorial scope of application. Thus, decentralisation seems to be
a more appropriate response to cover part of the needs of economic and
social agents, those that can be called proximity needs, to which regional
authorities seem to provide a greater response.
In view of the above, it can be concluded that certain problems requi-
re an international or supranational vision that leads to actions under
this perspective (thinking and acting globally, as proposed by environ-
mentalism in 1972), while other problems require actions that have a
certain relationship of territorial proximity with the citizen, and this has
been provoked by regional and local politics.
Issues related to the external sphere of the economy have until not so
long ago had a residual character in the analysis of economic politics,
given the low level of internationalisation of the economy, which meant
that national economic politics was designed basically in response to
domestic variables, although its independence was not complete. Howe-
ver, from the 1980s onwards, the interdependence of economies reached
such levels that there are now many voices that point out that the foreign
sector is configured as the central nucleus in which the potentialities and
restrictions of any national economy are concentrated.
Regardless of the trends set by citizens in the democratic exercise of
"one vote, one person", of the economic decisions taken by their govern-
ments and of the good performance of an economy, it is financial inves-
tors who determine in an important way the politics to be pursued at the
national level, otherwise confidence in the country in question will be
lost.
Taking advantage of this fact, the economic horizon is broadened
through trade and financial relations with other countries, which in-
fluences almost all economic variables: consumption, savings, inves-
tment, employment, resource allocation, prices, competitiveness.... This
being so, international economic relations place insurmountable cons-

7. Specific situations
Economic politics refers to the intention to intervene under a work
plan or programme, which is projected for the immediate or distant fu-
ture. Its execution is conditioned to alterations inherent to the logical
micro-impacts of confronting what is planned with reality. At times, spe-
cific situations arise that affect specific economic politics strategies more
intensely, and at other times the economic politics as a whole.
Sometimes the very announcement of an economic politics strategy
causes such a change in reality that its implementation becomes unne-
cessary. At other times it accentuates the problem and it is necessary to
reinforce or revise the intensity of action, as happens, for example, when
future interest rate hikes are announced due to excess liquidity and the
inflationary pressures that this generates; this will lead economic agents
to be more attentive to requesting more current debt before the hike,
accentuating liquidity and tensions.
There are problematic phenomena, events or situations that arise
unexpectedly but which are quite common or repetitive depending on
the territory, and although they are taken into account in planning (hen-
ce the incorporation of essential hypotheses), they require adaptation to
each circumstance, altering both the effort to be made and the initial le-
vels of success to be renounced. Thus, certain meteorological anomalies
(tornadoes, typhoons, hurricanes, etc.), non-meteorological natural phe-

9. Debate on conjunctural versus structural politics
LEconomic politics can be approached using only a temporal crite-
rion, distinguishing between that which seeks to achieve short-term
(conjunctural) objectives and that which focuses on long-term (structu-
ral) objectives, based on Kirschen's classification of objectives, albeit up-
dated. It is, in any case, widely used, both at the academic level and in
the practical or informative exercise of economic politics. There is
growing confusion about this differentiation, which in some cases is
even affecting the doctrine, which is infected by messages as loud as
"this recession is not cyclical but structural, and can only be resolved by
applying structural politics", which logically no one clearly states, most
probably because no one specifies whether this aggregate or microeco-
nomic supply crisis is due to this or that event.
Economic politics objectives require political action with a specific
direction, and this can be broadly classified into economic stabilisation
or conjunctural politics and economic development or structural poli-
tics.
The first assumes that aggregate demand is constituted as a specific
objective on which specific actions are applied, causing changes in the
quantity or composition of aggregate demand, and thus affecting the
objectives of price stability of demand and full employment of capacity
for cyclical reasons. This politic is developed by Keynes' General Theory,
although it will be discussed in more detail in later sections. This stabi-
lising politics, also called counter-cyclical, is completely linked to the
political intention of stabilising the economic cycle, and thus achieving
sustained economic growth.
Development or structural politics is aimed at controlling the long-
term objectives of the economy (external balance, economic growth, in-
come distribution, etc.), which implies changes in the structure of the
economy, and therefore usually responds to microeconomic and aggre-
gate supply problems that require more or less in-depth action, and

1. Sustained Economic Growth
Economic growth is a growing response in the development of supply
capacities to the demands of demand, so that the evolution of it, whether

to the open market. The marking of such growth as sustained or cyclical

with the objective of price stability: growth above this level generates in-

up), which implies certain tensions on the economic cycle; growth below
this level implies disinflationary tensions and reduces the capacity to ge-
nerate employment. The objective of economic politics is to achieve sus-
tained growth, as this removes the constraining elements that distort its
future potential, neither above nor below this level.
The concept of sustained growth is linked to the existence of econo-
mic cycles: effective production responds to variations in demand, thus
marking a downward path in phases of recession or an upward path in
phases of expansion, producing deviations from the objective of sustai-
ning growth. The market, whether free or intervened, behaves in a way
that is difficult to justify, with variations in demand in terms of quantity
or composition that are difficult to explain, but which find a response in
supply, whether domestic or foreign, with a view to obtaining a profit.
Supply is governed by a greater or lesser level of competition, showing

2. A model of economic growth based on debt
as a driver of economic recessions
The Central Bank of each territory is the entity entrusted with the
task of providing the system with sufficient and necessary liquidity, de-

controlling that the system itself drives an increase in liquidity through
the generation of bank liquidity and the changes that occur in the velo-

household, corporate and government indebtedness is boosted, so that
if this increases, so does liquidity. From there, the mechanisms used by
the CB to generate the necessary and sufficient additional liquidity will

the economy.

4. External balance
The external balance of an economy is a notion that goes beyond the
simple accounting balance of the balance of payments; it is broader and
more complex, as it also requires a certain stability over time. The two
instruments that allow us to know the value of a country's transactions
with the rest of the world and their impact on our economy are the ba-
lance of payments and the exchange rate, and although the former offers
us more detailed information, the exchange rate is acquiring clear im-
portance, given the magnitude of capital movements at the international
level, indicating the short-term influence of the foreign sector on the
economy.
It would be interesting for a territory to assess on a reduced regular
basis, if possible on a weekly or daily basis, the very extensive informa-
tion recorded in the goods and services account, as well as in the income,
capital and financial accounts. It is essential to have information on ex-
ternal flows, since it is a reflection of the decisions taken by residents
and non-residents on goods, services, income and capital, showing
changes in the behaviour of these transactions, and should contribute to
economic politics decisions.
The sum of the current and capital account balances represents a net
lending (surplus) or net borrowing (deficit) of the economy vis-à-vis the
rest of the world, which helps to define the international investment
position between the beginning and the end of the period. The balance
of payments is in balance when the overall balance of official reserves is
zero or small.
It is an objective which goodness is difficult to define, because de-
pending on the specific circumstances of the economic system and its
needs, a balance can be positive, but also negative, such as that obtained
through its financing through loans or short-term capital, or that which
is achieved through the impediment of the coverage of human needs
due to the lack of payment possibilities. On the other hand, an external
deficit may be positive, such as the so-called capitalisation deficit, but it
may not be positive; in any case, it implies a diversion of domestic de-
mand towards the exterior. Finally, the fact that a country has an external
surplus implies an imbalance to which little attention is paid, given that
it does not pose external payment difficulties; however, if it is perma-
nent, it generates inflationary tensions, appreciating its exchange rate
and, furthermore, such a situation implies that a country shows its re-

e) Stable employment and temporary employment. The former is
often understood as a desired situation and the latter as a problem to be
corrected. However, employment stability can introduce elements of a
brake on labour productivity, while at the same time placing limits on
free competition by restricting labour mobility, as well as consumption
and investment choices. On the other hand, temporary employment
need not be a problem. It will depend on the dynamism of the labour
market, since if it is high, temporariness can even be personally and
professionally enriching, especially for younger workers. A society must
analyse the reality of its labour market before acting in a particular di-
rection, because this can put a strain on economic growth.
The problem needs to be analysed more closely in two possible sce-
narios:
Situation A. Economy close to full employment and with high em-
ployment-generating dynamism.
A1. If there were a commitment to employment stability, this could
mean a high restriction on individual job growth, since the re-
sistance to losing this indefinite nature in hiring would weaken
the intentionality of change in the workforce. This resistance to
change would slow down the options of covering employment
needs in new activities with future potential as opposed to tho-
se that are becoming stagnant. Society loses potential for the
future, while employed people would be the group that would
benefit the most, as they would gain greater security.
A2. With a predominance of temporary employment, employed
people would be the most disadvantaged group, as they would
lose this stability, although as there is a high dynamism in job
creation the problem is less intense. Society would benefit in
terms of flexibility in the face of any change brought about by
the modernisation of the business and productive structure of
a territory, favouring the transfer of labour from sectors with
little future to others that do have a future.
Situation B. Economy in a situation of high unemployment and low
employment-generating dynamism.
B1. If there were a commitment to employment stability, this would
make it more difficult for employed and unemployed people to

Stability generates greater obligations to pay compensation, so
that companies will not be predisposed to change a person hi-

B2. With a predominance of temporary employment, relief is less

Possibly neither, but rather the dynamism of the labour market and the

1. If this is a market-wide problem, a strategy must be devised in

delimited in time and space. Once these causes are known, it will
be necessary to decide whether or not to intervene on them. One

school age and faster retirement, or due to the existence of groups
that withdraw from the labour supply. In these circumstances, a
strategy to solve the problem can be pursued, involving a num-
ber of possible options and combinations of them. Choices will
have to be made that are compatible with the ideological approa-

Finally, it should be pointed out that one of the problems that most
concerns a society is its capacity to generate employment, its economic
dynamism sufficient to meet the existing labour supply up to full emplo-
yment levels. If this is not sufficient, unwanted unemployment is gene-
rated, which will be identified with capacity unemployment, because it
shows us the greater or lesser capacity of an economy to generate suffi-
cient employment to meet labour supply and demand. Its variation may
be due to cyclical or structural causes:
1.º Structural reasons. The variation in the number of unemplo-
yed people is linked to certain activities and/or regions, and is
the result of technological and economic changes, which imply
an inadequate economic structure, based on difficulties for sec-
toral and geographical mobility, technological or productive
structure changes, business relocation, company mergers or
amalgamations, strong migratory attraction of certain geogra-
phical areas, increase in labour factor costs or a strong increase
in productivity, among many other causes. The unemployment
generated could be called structural, technological, migratory...
2.º Conjunctural reasons. One of the clear and immediate conse-
quences of going through a recessionary stage of the economic
cycle is an increase in unemployment, as a consequence of the
reduction in economic activity (redundancies and a reduction
in hiring); the beginning of the expansionary phase usually ge-
nerates employment, hence the reduction in unemployment.
Unemployment caused during a recession is called cyclical
unemployment; this is the type of unemployment that Keyne-
sians focus on, justifying it on the grounds of insufficient de-
mand, since they consider wages to be rigidly downward.
There are two additional circumstances that generate certain levels of
unemployment, both of which are compatible with a situation of full em-
ployment, although there may be times when a major problem may arise
in certain territories.
The first of these refers to frictional unemployment, which includes
first-time job seekers or those who have lost or left their job and need a
new one; given the imperfections of the labour market, this requires a
more or less long-term commitment. This rate will depend on the degree

6. Price stability
This is an essential objective for economic politics in a territory since
there is no defined International Monetary System (IMS). Once the do-
llar-gold standard disappears, the No-IMS means that confidence in a
currency becomes essential to optimise the positive impact of the existing
liquidity and the new liquidity injected into the growing exchanges that
generate income, wealth and employment. This capacity of the currency
is diminished in the absence of confidence. Thus, liquidity growth may
or may not be accepted, and its impact on economic dynamism and pri-
ces may be more appropriate, if confidence in the currency is high. The
behaviour of prices represents the extent to which the injection of liquidi-
ty is adequate or exceeds or falls below the sufficient and necessary level.
Prior to the 1970s, with the SMI, little attention was paid to this ob-
jective, and even situations of moderate inflation were accepted for their
positive effects, since they generated a monetary illusion and appeased
social demands; they stimulated investment by favouring the self-finan-
cing and indebtedness capacities of companies and generated tax reve-
nues for the State, since higher prices implied higher revenue through
consumption and income taxes.
Since the declaration of non-convertibility of the dollar into gold, ac-
companied by the oil shocks of the 1970s, the fight against inflation has
become a major objective. Moreover, it is only in mid-2021 that the need
seems to have been recognised that, on specific occasions, economic po-

each territory and at each moment in time, even if the figure coincides.
Price stability is, in fact, the ultimate objective of monetary politics,
and can be defined as a sustained increase in the general price level, ac-
cepting a rate of price growth which does not disrupt the functioning of
the economy. Moreover, its medium-term sustainability is intended to be
centred in terms of price stability of demand, allowing for certain increa-

medium term is gaining ground and calls for not acting on liquidity
when downward or upward tensions are not due to a major change in
aggregate demand. If a territory is around this level, it is said to be in a
situation of price stability; if it moves away from this level, it is inflation;
if it is below this level, it is disinflation.
Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon, so that
an excess of monetary liquidity in the market leads to an increase in
aggregate demand that is more relaxed with prices, and supply responds
by growing prudently and raising prices. In an environment of scarce
iquidity, it is difficult for inflationary pressures to arise. However, despi-
te this general recognition, there are other variables whose behaviour
generates inflationary and/or disinflationary pressures depending on
the time. It is important to recognise which events or phenomena cause
them, so that the sum of all these tensions is what leads to price stability,
inflation or disinflation. Thus, in addition to the demand factors to be
corrected by monetary politics, cost or structural factors (basic, circum-
stantial, cumulative or as a result of propagation mechanisms) can be
identified which should not be resolved by monetary politics, but which,
depending on the reason for their occurrence, should be addressed by
monetary politics.

1. The rationale for mixed stabilisation politics
As formulated in (1) and (7), an economic system, depending on its
economic dynamism at any given time, must continuously "manufacture"
new money, and the corresponding Central Bank is the entity entrusted
with the task of providing the system with sufficient and necessary liqui-
dity. From there, the mechanisms used to generate this growing liquidity
will have a logical impact on the general behaviour of the economy,
through aggregate demand, trying to:
— Contribute to more contained growth when it grows too much,
causing real growth to be above cyclical potential growth and
leading to inflationary pressures that jeopardise short-term objec-
tives and require action to trigger disinflationary pressures to off-
set them.
— Or by trying to boost it when aggregate demand levels are weak,
marking a trend of real growth below cyclical potential growth
with disinflationary pressures that jeopardise short-term objecti-
ves and require actions that provoke inflationary pressures to off-
set them.
The Governing Council of the ECB in 2021, when reviewing its mone-
tary politics strategy, sets the price stability objective more decisively at a
year-on-year increase in the Harmonised Index of Consumer Prices
(HICP) for the euro area of 2% over the medium term, recognising that it
is symmetric in nature as set out above. Deviations both below and above
the reference are equally undesirable, so it is essential to adapt monetary

politics to each territory and time, with all the characteristics and circum-
stances that will define it.
It is essential to inject liquidity in an increasing manner, but it is es-
sential to establish a limit to this increase in the generation of liquidity;
the limiting element lies in price stability. As we have seen when explai-
ning the case of Venezuela, what would be the point of generating, for
example, X billion more monetary units in a year, if the existing econo-
mic dynamisms are not capable of absorbing the new liquidity in terms
of income, wealth and employment generation, causing an inflationary
situation and thus a loss of purchasing power over all the existing liqui-
dity equivalent, for example, to (X+5) billion monetary units. This would
be absurd, because it would impoverish the economic system. The op-
posite is also absurd, not injecting possible liquidity, given these econo-
mic dynamisms and the existence of a need in terms of income, wealth
and employment; this would imply a lower than desired price level,
which would lead to a risk of lower economic dynamism and even an
economic recession. Therefore, the basic objective for monetary politics
is to achieve price stability by providing the system with liquidity that
does not exceed limits either below or above what is required at any gi-
ven time to achieve price stability.
Earlier we defined economic growth as an increasing response in the
development of supply capacities to the requirements of demand, so
that the evolution of it, whether national or international, has a clear
impact on the real response of supply to the open market. In other
words, as long as growing exchanges are generated in an economic sys-
tem, economic growth will be a reality, and if they are produced at the
desired levels, so will economic growth. However, for it to be sustained,
the liquidity injected into the system in an increasing manner must be
conditional, as indicated above, on the achievement of the price stability
objective.
Conjunctural politics seeks to bring about changes in aggregate de-
mand, both in composition, in order to make economic growth more
robust, thereby achieving better outcomes in terms of price stability and
full employment, and in quantity, in order to keep economic growth at
sustained levels.
The main instruments used to carry out this stabilising politics are
executed daily and are monetary, with a more accentuated participation
of fiscal and budgetary instruments in times of recession, as long as their
use is not conditional on austerity, and this is supported in a residual
way by direct control policies that qualify specific situations. The injec-

policy will be to try to withdraw or inject liquidity into the market, either

Through monetary politics, the aim is to influence the reserves or
quantity of money in the system and, therefore, the credit capacity of
commercial banks: easy money, hard money; in such a way that by in-
creasing or decreasing the liquidity of the system or the price of money,
the levels of credit demand are affected and, as a result, consumption
and investment patterns are influenced, especially those that are linked
to bank financing. This has an impact on income levels, employment
and price stability, which are fundamental objectives of monetary poli-
tics:
— An easy money or less contractionary monetary policy is one that ge-
nerates or facilitates liquidity at levels at which the market is de-
manding, and if possible even above. This is done by increasing
the amount of money in circulation and/or lowering interest ra-
tes in order to facilitate easier lending. This initiative is proposed
at times when the economic cycle is in its recessionary or decele-
rating phase, so that prices are growing below the desired level or
are falling. This easy money policy allows for more economic ac-
tivity, growth and employment, and generates more inflationary
pressures.
— A hard money or more contractionary monetary policy, involving con-
tainment of the quantity of money and/or higher interest rates
than the market is demanding, provides a brake on excessive
growth in aggregate demand, thereby controlling inflationary
pressures and containing activity within desired margins of sus-
tained growth.
— A monetary policy of continuity when price stability is a medium-
term reality, irrespective of the existence of inflationary and dis-
inflationary pressures that are fully or almost fully offset. The
economic system and its aggregate demand are growing adequa-
tely and the action should be to maintain the reference tender
rates and keep up the pace of injecting additional liquidity into
the economic system.

In practice, in an economy where the central objective of monetary
politics is price stability, this intervention is continuously contractionary
in nature because, although more liquidity is injected into the system, it
must at all times be below market demand, tightening the market and
preventing excess demand for liquidity from turning into excessive ag-
gregate demand, which in turn leads to higher prices. Only when infla-
tion is at dangerously low levels can the central bank afford to pursue a
less contractionary or easy money policy.
This way of implementing monetary politics makes it predictable, in
the sense that when an instrument starts to be used in a certain direc-
tion, economic agents anticipate the sequence of movements that will
occur in the relevant economic variables, which positively influences
the speed with which the effects are transmitted. Moreover, when there
is a shock to a relevant variable, agents know in advance which direc-
tion monetary politics will take.
However, this idea is the opposite of that held by the rational expec-
tations position, which indicates that the absolute predictability of mo-
netary politics makes it useless, since individuals anticipate its effects to
the point that they cancel it out; this is why this current is opposed to
monetary politics, considering it ineffective and, in any case, suggests
taking surprise measures that have effects because they cannot be anti-
cipated.

The proper functioning and behaviour of monetary politics is essen-

impact of its currency is appropriate and favours, at all times, generating
sufficient capacity to guarantee a sustained growth path. To this end, the
currency must reach economic agents in a more balanced way in order
to generate positive economic dynamism, with the capacity to generate
sufficient confidence. On the basis of this confidence, the impact of de-
mand on prices must become the limit that a central bank must set itself
in order to generate liquidity, so that the very dynamism of the economy
will determine its capacity to absorb more liquidity without affecting
price stability.
The traditional way of conducting monetary politics has been through
the financial system, by means of money auctions that reduce or increase
liquidity, depending on the behaviour of the overnight interbank market,
gradually increasing liquidity, so that the financial system transferred this

term) or seasonal credit (up to one year). Monetary politics has to be tailo-

aggregate demand, disinflationary tensions and a possible economic recession.

liquidity as an alternative to debt can reduce the intensity and frequency
of economic recessions, as it does not condition it on continued pressure
on economic agents to accumulate more debt.

1. Economic development and its model
Earlier we differentiated between stabilisation politics and economic
development politics. However, we have also mentioned that economic
politics is a whole (Figure 2.1). We have seen how stabilising politics is
that which tries to stabilise the business cycle and the achievement of
short-term objectives (conjunctural politics), usually through aggregate
demand. Economic development politics tries to achieve a desired path
for the primary objectives of the economy, through microeconomic or
aggregate supply politics. Thus, for M. Todaro "development should be
conceived as a multidimensional process composed of major transfor-
mations of social structures, people's attitudes and national institutions,
as well as the acceleration of economic growth, the reduction of inequa-
lity and the eradication of absolute poverty" (1988, p. 119).
The 1990s saw the emergence of a new term coined by the United
Nations, human development, which is defined as "the process of enlar-
ging the possibilities available to individuals: to live longer and in good
health, to be educated and to have resources that allow a decent stan-
dard of living are fundamental requirements (...) The process of develo-
pment should at least create an enabling environment that gives indivi-
duals and communities a chance to realise their potential and to lead
creative and productive lives in accordance with their needs and inter-
ests" (1990, p. 1). The individual is at the centre of development, as a
component of society, and this is achieved through the development of
the individual's skills, increasing the range of opportunities available to
individuals. At the heart of this concept are the individual, the enabling
135

The end of national economic crises
environment, potentialities, opportunities and choices, the more impor-
tant the closer the territorial environment is to the individual.
The ECS-ECM model considers development as the process that esta-
blishes and interrelates the formation of economic, social and cultural capabili-
ties that enable the individual, as part of a society, to evolve in the sense of a
prior mental abstraction.
Thus, from the mental conception reached by a society about its fu-
ture development, once the present situation has been accepted and as-
sumed, the need arises to make certain structural and institutional chan-
ges, which must provoke this shift towards capacities that allow the
achievement of this previously conceived development; in this process,
the public sector must represent the main instrument of society to reach
the set objective, but not become the decisive element.
The pursuit of economic development is so broad and complex that
it requires the involvement of society as a whole, not only to achieve it,
but also to realise its own approach. This gives the behaviour of indivi-
duals an essential role in this development process, as they set their own
development model. In the same sense, J. K. Galbraith (1994, p. 150) es-
tablishes as the first requirement for economic development to take pla-
ce in a country that there is a socially and economically motivated popu-
lation. This motivation will push the country towards human and
sustainable development.
In view of this new concept, development involves the design, by the
individual and the collectives in which it is included, at least in the ma-
jority, of a desired model of development, particular and specific to each
society, with its own particularities, which will ultimately mark distan-
ces from other models, or not, and will be decisive in providing it with
the necessary institutions for its achievement and proper functioning. It
is true that globalisation seems to lead us to similar goals, but reality
shows us singularities between nations, between regions, between loca-
lities and between groups of people.
The development process does not always respond to a pre-establis-
hed and identified model. Sometimes the model underlies society and
sets a path for development politics strategies. Other times, develop-
ment takes place in one direction or another, in the image of some deve-
lopment model or on the fringes of any known model, but this is the
inertia produced by actions that derive from partial objectives that try to
solve problems, such as improving education, health, employment con-
ditions...

2. Specificities and constraints for development politics
Stabilising politics is an action that aims to provide the system with
sufficient and necessary liquidity according to its economic dynamism,
for which price stability becomes the line on which it must be sustai-
ned in the medium term. Once liquidity is provided for the economic
system to function, it must show its achievements in terms of develo-
pment, which must generate dynamising impulses at the citizen, sec-
toral and territorial levels. This development politics is much more
varied and is closely linked to the singularities of each marked model,
to confidence in institutions or capacities and to existing roots. Logica-
lly, the design of strategies to define which institutions or capacities to
use to achieve the model is relatively simple, given that a sufficiently
skilled body of technicians has arrived in the XXI century to resolve it.
What is difficult is to confront the citizenry and their behaviour, which
is not always rational, is marked by roots and has more or less solid
levels of trust. It is sometimes hard work to achieve uprootedness and
trust. We have seen how governments have acted very aggressively in
the face of the pandemic, regardless of the actions taken, and it is true
that they have not always been coherent decisions, but neither has the
public's response been adequate.
A series of elements will be listed that are essential for the ECS-

ting it to each moment and territory, but above all, completing it with
the singularities of each model.

12] Guaranteeing a public pension system.
The structure of public pensions and their universality, the coverage
of which has been extended to all social groups, is well established in
most developed economies. The mechanism is that contributions deri-
ved from labour relations confer the right to a retirement pension, which
does not necessarily imply the recovery of what has been paid, but is
dependent on it, by virtue of the law that exists at the time. This amount
is made up of a fixed sum plus another portion related to the contribu-
tions paid and the time over which it is paid. However, current income
covers current payments regardless of future scheme liabilities.

A number of factors have contributed to the growth of pension sche-
mes and raised many doubts about their future. These are the dissolu-
tion of traditional family structures and the increasing longevity of reti-
rees; the absence of savings and investment opportunities that provide
a guaranteed and sufficient real return in private schemes; the insuffi-
ciency, real or perceived, of voluntary savings by individuals for retire-
ment age; the decision to provide equal and sufficient incomes for all
individuals, groups and generations, which has prompted governments
to guarantee minimum incomes for certain groups more generally and
political considerations that generate commitments in the form of a res-
ponse to future social needs.
For all these reasons, pensioners' income levels have improved
greatly in recent decades both in absolute terms and relative to the ave-
rage earnings of the working population. A significant proportion of
people have been able to retire before the normal retirement age provi-
ded for in state pension schemes, and few are now forced to live with
family members for financial reasons.
At present, the debate on pensions is based on their sustainability,
although this is not a permanent approach, the information that the pu-

payments in some economies is a problem of demographic transition,
given that 6-7 decades ago, depending on the country, there was strong
population growth due to family traditions and a sharp decline in infant
mortality. For example, in Spain, infant mortality began to fall in 1965
and since then has not stopped falling, so that in 1977 it was half of what
it was in 1964. In the case of Germany, the fall started earlier, so that in
1972 it was already less than half of what it was in 1960, and between
1978-79 it was three quarters lower. In the EU-27 average, it was half as
low in 1975 as it was in 1961 (Eurostat, December 2013). This caused the
population to grow very significantly during a decade and a half, which
clashes with the subsequent and current trend, in which families have
reduced their birth rate very significantly. In global terms there is a very
significant population growth in a corseted period, combined with a fa-
lling birth rate, and this is visible in the population pyramids of many
countries.

taining the pension system with such a large contingent of people as
they reach the age in question, for a longer period of time and with a
higher income base due to higher wages. To pay for this, even if there is
a labour turnover through an influx of working-age migrants, requires a

ECS-MEC MODEL CONCLUSIONS
Society is changing at a rapid pace. People are aware of this, but not
so much of the change that is originating in people and its impact on the
model of operation of the economy, regardless of whether our beha-
viour is rational or irrational, and it will be so to very different degrees
depending on which variable, fact, phenomenon... we act on, and de-
pending on the moment and on which people. This is why it is neces-
sary to change the intervention model, which is so neutral, so unhuma-
nistic, and to build a model based on a logical, but above all human,
sense. Hence the insistence on recovering three variables that are essen-
tial to the proper functioning of the economic system: movement, effort
and trust, both in the exercise of our private economic activities and in
understanding the role of economic politics intervention in the eco-
nomy.
As we move further into this century, there is a growing and signifi-
cant decline in all three variables, so that the population begins to find
multiple justifications for not moving or moving less; it increasingly de-
sires less effort in favour of idle personal time and, at the same time,
shows a greater degree of mistrust in what is alien, in all different thin-
king, especially in the institutions created, in the policies developed and
in those in charge of designing and executing them. There is a biological
determinism (social and economic genetic map) that seems to prioritise
the hedonistic postulates defended by a part of society; the opposite po-
sition that puts territories before people is not correct either. The new
model that is being proposed seeks a clear commitment to a common
approach that reinforces at every step the capacity for movement, effort
and trust, as the keys to each of the interventions in economic politics,
whether in terms of individual measures or complete strategies.

The main intention of this paper is to expose the doctrine's much-
argued necessity of economic recessions linked to medium-wave busi-
ness cycles. They are simply a consequence of the existing economic
growth model. If the model is changed to a different one, the consequen-
ces can be quite different, even eliminating these economic crises.
Under a new economic concept, it has been shown that in a sovere-
ign economic system there is a need to generate liquidity, which must be
provided in the necessary and sufficient quantity for it to function pro-
perly. Through the dynamism generated by the different economic
agents, the increase in liquidity is translated into prices and economic
growth to an adequate degree. Trade therefore grows at a faster rate to
the extent that the payment instrument in use is sufficient, dynamic and,
above all, that it is growing and necessary. This implies that liquidity
must grow continuously if sustained economic growth is to be guaran-
teed. Thus, a new quantitative theory of money is presented with a di-
fferent starting point: we speak of the creation of money out of necessity,
that of guaranteeing sustained economic growth. This liquidity has a
real monetary impact of liquidity in the system: µ = (1 − π)(M + L + L )

But the economic crises that most concern society respond to a logic
of growing, continuous indebtedness, when the system is unable to ac-

phenomenon of indebtedness the basis of the model of economic growth
in modern economies, despite the fact that the role of bank money in the
economy has been amply studied and the monetary instruments used
by the main Central Banks are well known. It has been shown how the
need to inject liquidity increasingly is essential for the functioning of the
economy; how this requires the need to generate impulses on the econo-

to do so, without the need to place the entire weight of intervention on
monetary politics. All this conventional monetary politics makes econo-
mic growth conditional on continued indebtedness and, as this is not
possible, when it reaches very high levels it ends up causing a preferen-
ce for liquidity, which translates into a slowdown or fall in aggregate
demand, disinflationary tensions and a possible economic recession.

late the liquidity of the system in terms of positive effects, always bea-
ring in mind inflationary or disinflationary demand pressures. In this
model, the economic system depends on the market and its ability to
generate increasing economic exchanges between individuals and their
collectives. Money becomes a key tool to which sufficiency, necessity,
dynamism and confidence must be demanded, and which must be "ma-
nufactured" to a greater or lesser extent depending on the required ag-

injecting liquidity an alternative to borrowing, which does so in a stable

amount to be injected. The advantages of this group have been cited as
